A Second Blog Post

November, 5 2026

Now that we have reached the halfway point of CSC 426, I would say that the midterm has been a pretty important wake-up call for me in terms of my contributions to the project and my overall development as a software engineer. Although I feel that I have made a reasonable amount of progress throughout the first half of the semester, I also recognize that there is still a lot of room for improvement, especially when it comes to my consistency and willingness to step outside of my comfort zone. One of the biggest things I have come to realize is that becoming more comfortable with open source software development requires actively involving myself in the development process rather than waiting until I feel completely prepared to make a contribution. While I have certainly learned a lot throughout the class so far, I believe that I could have learned much more had I been more consistent with my contributions and taken on unfamiliar challenges more frequently.

In terms of what has gone well, I would say that my familiarity with the development workflow and my confidence when navigating the repository have improved considerably. Earlier in the semester, I found myself somewhat overwhelmed by the number of contributions being made and the amount of unfamiliar code I had to navigate. However, as I have spent more time working with the repository and interacting with other contributors, I have started to become more comfortable with the collaborative aspects of open source software development. For example, I recently participated in reviewing PR #175 on October 26th and PR #174 on November 2nd. These contributions have helped me become more familiar with the code review process, which is an important aspect of software engineering that I haven't had much exposure to outside of this class. Reading through other people's contributions also gives me an opportunity to understand different approaches to solving problems and potentially learn techniques that I can apply to my own work in the future.

However, one aspect of my performance that hasn't gone as planned is the consistency of my contributions. Although I have made progress and become more comfortable with the development environment, I feel that I could have been contributing much more frequently throughout the semester. I think one of the biggest reasons for this is my tendency to spend too much time considering whether or not I am prepared to take on a particular issue, especially when it involves technical concepts that I am unfamiliar with. While I understand the importance of choosing issues that are appropriate for my current skill level, I also recognize that constantly limiting myself to familiar tasks can slow down my development as a software engineer. In retrospect, I believe that approaching unfamiliar issues with a willingness to learn, rather than viewing my lack of experience as a reason to avoid them, would have been much more beneficial to my growth.

Another factor that has affected my progress is time management. Although I have been able to balance my responsibilities in CSC 426 with my other classes and commitments, I haven't always been intentional about setting aside time specifically for open source contributions. As a result, I sometimes find myself going several days without making meaningful progress on the project, which makes it more difficult to maintain momentum. I believe this is something that is entirely within my control, and improving it would allow me to become more involved in the project while also creating more opportunities to develop my technical skills.

With that in mind, one specific habit I want to change for the second half of the semester is committing to making at least two meaningful contributions per week. I believe that establishing this baseline would help me remain consistently involved with the project while also encouraging me to explore issues and responsibilities that I might otherwise avoid. These contributions wouldn't necessarily have to involve resolving an issue every single time, since reviewing pull requests, providing feedback, and participating in discussions are also important aspects of open source software development. However, I do want to make sure that I am actively contributing to the development of the application rather than simply observing the work being done by others.

In order to implement this change, I plan to dedicate specific periods throughout each week to reviewing the repository, identifying issues that I can work on, and making progress toward my contributions. Ideally, I would like to complete at least one contribution during the first half of the week and another during the second half, which would help me avoid putting everything off until the last minute. I also want to make a conscious effort to take on tasks that involve concepts I am unfamiliar with, even if that means spending additional time researching documentation, asking questions, or seeking guidance from other contributors. Ultimately, I want to use the remainder of CSC 426 as an opportunity to become a more independent and confident software engineer, and I believe that developing a consistent contribution habit will be an important step toward achieving that goal.

As of my previous reflection, I had reported seven story points across two issues. Since October 22nd, my contribution log does not show any additional merged pull requests, meaning that I haven't recorded any new completed story points during this period. However, I have continued participating in the project through other forms of contribution, particularly pull request reviews.

Specifically, I participated in reviewing PR #175 on October 26th and PR #174 on November 2nd. Although these contributions do not directly increase my completed story point total, I still consider them important to my development as a software engineer because they allow me to become more familiar with the code written by other contributors and the process of evaluating changes before they are incorporated into the application. I believe that code reviews are especially valuable in an open source environment since the quality and maintainability of the project depend heavily on contributors being willing to examine each other's work and provide meaningful feedback.

In terms of why my completed story point total hasn't increased during this period, I would primarily attribute it to the inconsistency in my contributions and my tendency to remain within my comfort zone. While reviewing pull requests has been a useful learning experience, I recognize that I need to balance these contributions with more hands-on development work if I want to continue improving my technical skills. Moving forward, I hope that my goal of making at least two contributions per week will help address this issue by encouraging me to remain actively involved in the project, take on more challenging issues, and gain more experience with both the technical and collaborative aspects of open source software engineering.

A First Blog Post

October 21, 2026

This class has been my first exposure to open source software development and so far, it has been a great introduction to the development style. Throughout the course, I have learned about the pros and cons of open source software development, its typical audience, and the typical workflows within such online spaces. Although I'm not fully adjusted to the development style just yet, I feel comfortable enough asking questions and seeking guidance for topics or practices I'm unfamiliar with and navigating relevant challenges I face in CSC 426.

Near the beginning of CSC 426, I found myself facing one of the major challenges with open source software development as a whole: large-scale collaboration. Specifically, navigating an online space in which everyone is able to edit each other's work, request changes, and make meaningful alterations to the trajectory of the project. Although this can be perceived as a positive aspect of open source software engineering, it does create a few complications. For example, the volume of contributions, to me, can feel fast-paced and somewhat overwhelming. In addition, keeping track of what contributions have already been made while trying not to create duplicate issues can be difficult at times, especially if it's an issue that can be worded in several different ways. However, I understand these challenges are natural and most likely faced by the majority of people who are new to open source software development. With that in mind, I embrace the unfamiliarity of this development style and hope to adjust more to it throughout the class and potentially in future projects I pick up.

In terms of positive aspects of open source software development, the community aspect is definitely the biggest factor that comes to mind. Although I mentioned some challenges that come from large-scale collaboration, the community is still one of the greatest parts of it. This type of development environment encourages people to actively communicate with others, review each other's work, and provide meaningful feedback that fosters a welcoming learning environment. The community aspect of open source software development is the core of the development style, and it's what leads to significant mental growth and learning opportunities. While the practice of making several changes to the same project with over a dozen other people, and even thousands for larger projects, can seem overwhelming at first, it ultimately creates a supportive development environment in which people are encouraged to seek out help from others while learning how to help others themselves. Open source software engineering has also proven to be an effective model for building critical software infrastructure, as seen in several industry-level software systems such as Linux, Android, and OpenStack. Software such as these are prime examples of how widespread open source can be, since there are enterprise-level systems being run by open source contributions. Although open source software engineering poses the risk of low-quality code being pushed, the existence of maintainers, code reviews, and open source guidelines solve this issue by setting a standard for contributions.

As of October 21st, 2026, I have completed 7 total story points across two resolved issues. The first issue I completed was a simple issue related to the color and icon of a warning message on one of BCSR's pages that unintentionally appeared to be an error message. I attributed 2 story points PR #137 because the fix was a simple visual alteration to a page's UI, spanning a few lines and only affecting two files. The other issue I solved was more complex, involving the database and email creation. I attributed 5 story points to PR #155 because the fix was a completely new feature that spans across two pages, uses GET requests to determine which faculty to send an email to based on which courses are missing syllabi. In retrospect, I would say the first PR was somewhat easy to complete, but the difficulty primarily came from adjusting to the existing code in the repo since I was unfamiliar with it at first. The second PR was comparatively much more difficult since it involved writing code that involved reading the database and producing different behavior based on the contents of the database, something I don't have much experience with generally.
