A First Blog Post

October 21, 2026

This class has been my first exposure to open source software development and so far, it has been a great introduction to the development style. Throughout the course, I have learned about the pros and cons of open source software development, its typical audience, and the typical workflows within such online spaces. Although I'm not fully adjusted to the development style just yet, I feel comfortable enough asking questions and seeking guidance for topics or practices I'm unfamiliar with and navigating relevant challenges I face in CSC 426.

Near the beginning of CSC 426, I found myself facing one of the major challenges with open source software development as a whole, large scale collaboration. Specifically, navigating an online space in which everyone is able to edit each other's work, request changes, and make meaningful alterations to the trajectory of the project. Although this can be percieved as a positive aspect of open source software engineering, it does create a few complications. For example, the volume of contributions, to me, can feel fast-paced and somewhat overwhelming. In addition, keeping track of what contributions have already been made while trying not to create duplicate issues can be difficult at times, especially if its an issue that can be worded in several different ways. However, I understand these challenges are natural and most likely faced by the majority of people who are new to open source software development. With that in mind, I embrace the unfamiliarity of this development style and hope to adjust more to it throughout the class and potentially in future projects I pick up.

In terms of positive aspects of open source software development, the community aspect is definetly the biggest factor that comes to mind. Although I mentioned some challenges that come from large scale collaboration, the community is still one of the greatest parts of it. This type of development environment encourages people to actively communicate with others, review each other's work, and provide meaningful feedback that fosters a welcoming learning environment. The community aspect of open source software development is the core of the development style, and it's what leads to significant mental growth and learning opportunities. While the practice of making several changes to the same project with over a dozen other people, and even thousands for larger projects, can seem overwhelming at first, it ultimately creates a supportive development environment in which people are encouraged to seek out help from others while learning how to help others themselves. Open source software engineering has also proven to be a effective model for building critical software infastructure as seen in several industry level softwares such as Linux, Android, and OpenStack. Softwares such as these are prime examples of how widespread open source can be, since there are enterprise-level systems being ran by open source contributions. Although open source software engineerng poses the risk of low quality code being pushed, the existance of maintainers, code reviews, and open source guidelines solve this issue by setting a standard for contributions.

As of October 21st, 2026, I have completed 7 total story points across two resolved issues. The first issue I completed was a simple issue related to the color and icon of a warning message on one of BCSR's pages that unintentionally appeared to be an error message. I attributed 2 story points PR #137 because the fix was a simple visual alteration to a page's UI, spanning a few lines and only affecting two files. The other issue I solved was more complex, involving the database and email creation. I attributed 5 story points to PR #155 because the fix was a completely new feature that spans across two pages, uses GET requests to determine which faculty to send an email to based on which courses are missing syllabi. In retrospect, I would say the first PR was somewhat easy to complete, but the difficulty primarily came from adjusting to the existing code in the repo since I was unfamiliar with it at first. The second PR was comparatively much more difficult since it involved writing code that involved reading the database and producing different behavior based on the contents of the database, something I don't have much experience with generally.
